Neurochip: an 8-core RRAM neuromorphic processor

VLSI course project, with Atharva Chougule

Built in eight phases. It starts with a transistor-level neuron in Cadence Virtuoso, moves to a digital leaky integrate-and-fire core with on-chip STDP learning, and ends as a multi-core tile joined by a network-on-chip. Swapping SRAM weight memory for RRAM crossbar macros cut the reported area by about 81%.

What was measured, and what is still open

A forward pass fell from about 530 cycles to 96 once a single-cycle vector-matrix multiply replaced the 16-chunk weight fetch. The 8-core design went through Yosys, OpenROAD and KLayout to a complete GDSII, with a round-robin network-on-chip carrying 9-bit AER spike packets.

The analog groundwork is in the same report: a neuron schematic and symbol, a two-neuron chain with SR latches, a hysteresis loop, and a resistor sweep. The schematic fired eight spikes per input pulse and the symbol six, which traced back to the input RC network.

Open points. Worst slack is −0.02 ns at 15 ns, and 41 layout violations remain (37 DRC, 4 antenna). RRAM is a behavioural model, so the 872 mW power figure leaves out RRAM power. A 16-core version passed RTL simulation but ran out of memory in place and route.

PWM motor controller with live throttle feedback

ASIC design lab, with Atharva Chougule

A closed-loop motor controller taken from RTL to GDSII with the Cadence flow: Genus for synthesis, Innovus for place and route, Tempus for timing sign-off, on a 45 nm library at 100 MHz. A fixed-point PI loop holds a speed setpoint from tachometer feedback, and a protection stage forces the outputs low on over-current or lost feedback.

Design and verification details

The design has a 16-register APB interface, a 16-bit PWM core with double-buffered duty updates, programmable dead-time between complementary outputs, a Q8.8 PI controller, soft-start, slew-rate limiting and duty clamps. The tachometer input passes a two-flop synchroniser and a four-sample glitch filter. Assertions check dead-time and fault-response latency. I also integrated it with a PID engine to test a sensor-to-motor chain.

Open points. Power is an estimate at default switching activity. 443 timing endpoints still need constraints. My Python golden model reached 80.35 against a target of 500 in its run, so the PI gains need tuning before the RTL and the model can be compared.

SequrX: QR-based visitor access for residential societies

Course project, with Atharva Chougule and Dhyan Patel

Residents issue time-bound QR passes, guards scan them at entry and exit from a phone browser, and each valid scan becomes a gate command that a Raspberry Pi turns into servo movement on a miniature gate. Residents get an in-app notification when a visitor enters.

Web app
Next.js on Vercel
Backend

Gate

How it fits together

The web app never talks to the hardware directly. It writes a command to Firestore and the Pi picks it up, so the database is the only link between software and gate and every command stays on record. Unknown QR codes are rejected: a payment QR scanned at the gate produced an ACCESS_DENIED record and the gate stayed closed.

Status. The resident and guard portals, entry and exit logging with durations, notifications and the gate prototype work. The administrator portal, WhatsApp pass delivery and analytics are still in progress. The QR code currently carries a plain pass ID, so signed payloads are the next security step.

A Siamese ResNet-18 combined with rotated ZNCC template matching to find wafer features in SEM images that have drifted, rotated, scaled or gone noisy. It is trained on synthetic FinFET SEM images, so it needs no proprietary fab data.

CPW resonator design automation

Independent project

A Python tool that turns RF parameters into a coplanar waveguide quarter-wave resonator: it computes characteristic impedance, effective dielectric constant, guided wavelength and resonator length, then generates the 3D geometry for simulation in COMSOL.

What it produces

Parametric CAD for the substrate, ground plane, feed line and resonator is built with CadQuery and exported as STEP, STL, BREP and JSON. PyVista gives a quick 3D preview before the geometry goes into COMSOL Multiphysics for electromagnetic simulation.

One amplifier design simulated in Cadence Virtuoso at 180 nm, 90 nm and 45 nm, with DC operating point, AC and transient analysis, to see what technology scaling does to gain, bandwidth, power and output swing.

What the comparison shows

The 180 nm node gave the highest gain and swing. The 90 nm node had the widest bandwidth and the lowest gain, which is the usual gain-bandwidth trade-off as output resistance drops. The 45 nm node used the least power. Future work is an op-amp, mismatch analysis, and layout with parasitic extraction.

Isospectrality of rationally extended quantum harmonic oscillators under PT-symmetric perturbations

Research paper with Prof. Velmathi Guruviah and Atharva Chougule. Manuscript under review.

Adding an imaginary linear term to a quantum harmonic oscillator makes the Hamiltonian non-Hermitian but PT-symmetric. We tested numerically whether the rational extensions of the oscillator keep their energy levels under that perturbation. They do: the spectrum stays real and identical for every extension order tested.

Energy levels
(n + ½)ω + γ²/ω²
Imaginary parts
≤ 10⁻¹⁴
Extension orders
m = 0, 1, 2
Method and findings

I diagonalised the Hamiltonian in a 60-dimensional truncated Fock basis for perturbation strength γ from 0 to 3, with ω = 2. No exceptional points, level crossings or degeneracies appeared. The energy shift is a uniform γ²/ω² that does not depend on the level n or the extension order m, while the eigenfunctions change substantially as m grows.

That lets a designer hold the spectrum fixed and use the extension order as a free parameter to reshape the eigenfunctions. Extending the study to 2D and 3D anisotropic oscillators, and to behaviour near the PT-breaking threshold, is the natural next step.

Low-cost digital stethoscope with machine-learning heart-sound classification

Signal Processing course project, with Atharva Chougule. The paper adds Satyakam Baraha and Saurav Gupta.

A stethoscope built end to end: an electret microphone and amplifier circuit capture heart sounds, a Python desktop app records and filters them, and a neural network classifies each recording as normal, murmur or artifact. The paper was presented at IEEE INDISCON 2026 at MNIT Jaipur and is scheduled for IEEE Xplore.

Overall accuracy
89.42%
Abnormal sounds
90.04%
Recordings
520
Hardware, software and how it improved

Hardware. An electret condenser microphone feeds LM358 and LM386 amplifier modules, followed by RC filters for a 20 to 150 Hz band and a 50 Hz notch, into an ESP32 ADC. Heart sounds are very low frequency, and an ordinary microphone struggles without a proper stethoscope chestpiece.

Software. A Tkinter app shows the live waveform, lets you pick the microphone, records heart or lung sounds for 10 to 60 seconds, adjusts the low-pass cutoff, displays BPM, and saves the raw and filtered audio. BPM comes from band-pass filtering, a Hilbert envelope, adaptive peak detection and RR-interval analysis.

Machine learning. The first model used about 700 labelled samples (normal, extrasystoles, murmurs) with MFCC and spectrogram features and reached about 73% accuracy. Background noise and a small dataset were the main limits. The version in the paper uses a 1D CNN on 120 MFCC-based features per frame (with delta and delta-delta) over 200-frame sequences, after a 50 Hz notch and a sixth-order Butterworth low-pass.
